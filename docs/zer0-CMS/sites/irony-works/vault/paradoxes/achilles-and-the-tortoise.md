# Achilles and the Tortoise

Zeno of Elea, arguing around 450 BCE in defence of Parmenides' claim that motion is impossible, produced arguments that survive only in Aristotle's summaries. The second of the four on motion gives the tortoise a head start and observes that Achilles must first reach the tortoise's starting point, by which time the tortoise has advanced; then reach *that* point, by which time it has advanced again; and so on without end. Every premise is true. Achilles is faster; he must pass through each point the tortoise occupied; there are infinitely many such points. The step that carries the argument to its conclusion is the one nobody questioned for a long time: that a task with infinitely many parts cannot be completed in finite time.

The step is false, and saying precisely why took about two thousand years. The stages are not equal — each is a fixed fraction of the last — and a geometric series of shrinking durations converges: one, plus a half, plus a quarter, and so on forever, is two, not infinity. Achilles passes the tortoise at exactly the moment the series sums to, and the infinitely many stages fit inside a finite interval because they are infinitely many *shrinking* stages. Aristotle had already seen the shape of the answer — the points are potentially infinite, divisible without limit, not actually infinite as a completed set — but the arithmetic that makes the answer a proof arrived with the calculus. This is Quine's own example of a [[zer0-CMS/sites/irony-works/vault/forms/falsidical|falsidical]] paradox: the conclusion is false, the fault is in the reasoning, and the paradox lasted exactly as long as the fault stayed hidden.

What the arithmetic resolves is the paradox as an argument against motion. What it does not resolve is the question the argument raises about completing infinitely many acts: Black (1951) argued that there is no last step and so no sense in which the sequence is finished, and Thomson (1954) built the lamp that is switched infinitely often in a finite time and asked whether it ends up on or off. Those are open, and they are why this entry's standing reads `resolved` with a caveat rather than `resolved` without one. The race is settled. The supertask is not.
